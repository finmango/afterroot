01

the problem

Here's what happens after a flood destroys your home. You're on a friend's couch. You've lost documents. Your kids need to get back to school. Your medication was in the house. And someone hands you a list of 14 different agencies to call.

You've got 60 days to file with FEMA before the window closes. But FEMA won't send an inspector until you've filed with your insurance company first. Meanwhile, you need to fill out an SBA disaster loan application — not because you want a loan, but because FEMA requires it before they'll consider you for certain grants. Miss it, and you lose access to FEMA personal property assistance entirely.

That's just the financial side. You also need emergency Medicaid because you lost your job and your employer-sponsored health insurance with it. Your kid's asthma meds need refilling but you don't know which pharmacies are open. FEMA funds Crisis Counseling programs, but nobody told you they exist. There are utility moratoriums, tax deadline extensions, mortgage forbearance options, Red Cross grants, Disaster Legal Services, and local nonprofit relief. Each one has its own rules. None of them talk to each other.

The result is what you'd expect. People miss deadlines. They leave money on the table. They skip medications. They take on debt they didn't need to. Or they just give up. The Urban Institute found that financial health keeps declining into the third and fourth year after a disaster. Not because the aid doesn't exist — because the system wasn't designed for someone whose life just fell apart.

02

this isn't three problems. it's one.

When a disaster hits, health, resilience, and economy collapse at the same time. The financial recovery timeline is the health timeline is the economic timeline. They're inseparable.

health

People lose employer health insurance when they lose jobs. Chronic conditions worsen when medications are gone. PTSD, depression, and substance abuse spike. FEMA funds crisis counseling — but most people never hear about it.

resilience

Financial recovery takes 3.5 to 6 years on average. Families paying rent on one home while rebuilding another. New aid materializes years later. Communities that can't recover financially can't recover at all.

economy

Every dollar of disaster aid that reaches a household flows into local businesses, prevents foreclosures, and keeps communities intact. Every dollar left on the table is economic damage that compounds for years.

Most disaster tools address one piece. Wildfire mapping tools help with planning. Emergency notification systems handle evacuation. But nobody is solving the financial recovery navigation problem — the thing that determines whether a family actually rebuilds or spirals into long-term poverty. That's the gap AfterRoot fills.

03

the solution

AfterRoot is the caseworker that doesn't exist yet. An AI that activates the moment a disaster is declared, figures out every program a household qualifies for — financial, health, food, legal — and walks them through the whole process.

layer one

crisis financial & health assessment

When a presidential disaster declaration drops, AfterRoot is ready within hours. A short conversation captures what happened, who's in the home, what insurance looks like, what medications are needed, what health coverage exists. The goal is to build a complete picture — financial and health — fast, because the clock is already ticking.

layer two

unified aid navigator

Instead of making someone call seven agencies, AfterRoot checks everything at once: FEMA grants, SBA loans, state emergency funds, insurance paths, emergency Medicaid, crisis counseling, SNAP expedited benefits, utility shutoff protections, mortgage forbearance, tax extensions, Red Cross, Disaster Legal Services, local nonprofits. One conversation, one complete picture.

layer three

application & claims assistant

This is where the real money gets left on the table. Most people don't know that FEMA won't process certain grants until you've been denied by the SBA. They don't know that filing insurance in the wrong order delays their FEMA inspection by months. AfterRoot handles all of it: pre-fills forms, tracks every deadline, manages the dependency chain between agencies, and sends reminders before anything expires.

layer four

recovery intelligence

Disaster recovery takes years. The average housing recovery timeline is 3.5 to 6 years. New funding shows up long after the news cameras leave — CDBG-DR grants can take years to materialize. Appeal windows open. New nonprofits enter the response. Health coverage options change. AfterRoot stays with households through all of it, sending alerts when new resources appear so nobody gets forgotten once the initial wave of attention passes.

why FinMango

this is what we already do

FinMango's whole thing is helping people navigate financial systems that are too complicated. We built a Food Assistance Calculator that checks SNAP eligibility across multiple programs at once, and a Food Desert Analyzer that maps where grocery access breaks down by zip code. Both are live, both have real users. AfterRoot takes that same approach — cross-referencing fragmented programs, surfacing what people qualify for, guiding them through applications — and points it at disaster recovery. Different problem, same muscle.

04

deployment scenarios

Every time the president declares a disaster, AfterRoot turns on. It reads the declaration — which counties, what type, which programs activated — and configures itself for that specific event. No manual setup. The disaster is the deployment trigger.

Hurricanes
The most financially tangled disasters. Was it wind or flood damage? That determines which insurance applies, which changes FEMA eligibility, which affects SBA referral. Add health insurance disruption on top. Most families can't untangle this alone.
Wildfires
When the house is gone entirely, the financial and health picture gets complicated fast. Replacement vs. repair, land contamination, temporary housing for years, respiratory health impacts, mental health crisis. State planners need to know where recovery is stalling.
Flooding
Only about 4% of U.S. homes have flood insurance, but flooding is the most common natural disaster. Most flood victims are hitting FEMA for the first time with zero experience. These are the people who need a guide most.
Tornadoes
Everything happens fast. The destruction is sudden, the deadlines start immediately, and there's no lead time to prepare. AfterRoot's auto-activation means households can start the recovery process within hours.
05

government partnership

AfterRoot works through the infrastructure that's already there. Emergency management agencies, Disaster Recovery Centers, 211 call lines, community health centers — these are the places people go after a disaster. Right now, those systems are overwhelmed every time. AfterRoot takes the financial and health navigation piece off their plate so caseworkers can focus on the cases that actually need a human.

emergency management

State and county EM agencies build AfterRoot into their disaster response playbook

disaster recovery centers

FEMA DRCs use it to triage survivors through the multi-agency maze

211 systems & health depts

Call centers and health departments handle financial + health recovery questions at scale

community organizations

VOADs, FQHCs, churches, and local nonprofits use it as a force multiplier for overloaded case managers

The math is simple for government: every household that gets through the system without needing in-person help saves hundreds of hours of staff time. Every deadline AfterRoot catches avoids a FEMA appeal that costs everyone.

06

measurable impact

speed
How fast do people get from disaster declaration to first check in hand? We'll measure against FEMA's existing timelines.
completion
How many more people finish their FEMA, SBA, Medicaid, and state applications? How many fewer get rejected for missing paperwork?
dollars found
Total aid accessed across all programs — financial, health, and food — compared to what similar households typically recover without help.
health continuity
How many households maintain health coverage and medication access through the disaster recovery period? Reduction in ER visits driven by coverage gaps.
equity
Are we closing the gap? FEMA's own data shows disparities by income, race, and geography. We'll track whether AfterRoot moves the needle.
07

scalability

AfterRoot's scaling works differently than most tools. The disaster itself is the trigger. New declaration, new counties, new program mix — but the same architecture underneath. The platform reads the declaration, pulls in the relevant programs, and goes live. No manual onboarding for each event.

There are 60+ presidential disaster declarations every year in the U.S. Each one is a deployment. And the system gets smarter with each disaster — learning which programs interact in unexpected ways, where people get stuck, what documentation trips people up.

Long-term, the model works anywhere disaster aid systems are fragmented — the EU Civil Protection Mechanism, Japan's Disaster Relief Act, India's National Disaster Response Fund. FinMango already works in 13+ countries, so the global network is there when it's time to expand.

08

about FinMango

FinMango is a 501(c)(3) I founded in 2017 to tackle the financial health problems that hit young adults and vulnerable communities hardest. What started as an idea at Kent State has grown into an organization that's reached over a million people across 13+ countries.

The thread through everything we do is the navigation problem. People are entitled to help that exists — but the systems delivering it are so fragmented that millions never access what they qualify for. We've built free tools to close that gap, including a Food Assistance Calculator and Food Desert Analyzer that are live right now. We've partnered with Google Health, the World Health Organization, the World Bank, and the IMF.

AfterRoot is the most urgent application of this work. When a family loses their home, their health coverage, and their financial stability all at once, the last thing they should have to fight is the complexity of the systems designed to help them.
